Equations and implementation

Particle motion and electric potential

Electrons and O⁺ ions move in a periodic 2D box and generate a shared electric field.

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm d\mathbf{x}_s}{\mathrm dt} &= \mathbf{v}_s, & \frac{\mathrm d\mathbf{v}_s}{\mathrm dt} &= \frac{q_s}{m_s}\,\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}_s,t),\\[0.6em] \mathbf{E} &= -\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi, & \nabla^2\phi &= -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0},\\[0.6em] \rho &= e(n_i-n_e). \end{aligned}\]

Here \(\mathbf x\) and \(\mathbf v\) are two-component vectors, \(q_e = -e\) and \(q_i = +e\). \(\phi\) is electric potential [V], \(\mathbf E\) is electric field [V/m], and \(\rho\) is charge density [C/m³]. The physical mass ratio is used for singly charged oxygen ions.

The scattered signal

The electrons’ scattering contributions add coherently:

\[a(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_p}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_p}\exp\!\left[-\mathrm{i}\,k_{\mathrm B}x_j(t)\right]\]

\(N_p\) is the number of simulated electrons; \(k_{\mathrm B}\) is the radar Bragg wave number. All simulated electrons contribute, not just the displayed dots.

The Debye-length view

\[\lambda_{\mathrm{De}} = \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_0 k_{\mathrm B}T_e}{n_e e^2}}\]

The camera shows a 10\(\lambda_{\mathrm{De}}\) × 10\(\lambda_{\mathrm{De}}\) region of the plasma. Particles cross the camera edges freely.

Theoretical spectrum

The ochre curve is the collisionless, unmagnetized Maxwellian prediction with the selected run's density, separate electron and ion temperatures, O⁺ mass and Bragg wave number. It includes finite Debye length and both ion and electron modes:

\[S(k,f)=\left|1-\frac{\chi_e}{\epsilon}\right|^2g_e(f)+\left|\frac{\chi_e}{\epsilon}\right|^2g_i(f),\qquad\epsilon=1+\chi_e+\chi_i.\]

Here \(g_s\) is the unit-area free-particle Doppler spectrum and \(\chi_s\) is each species' Vlasov susceptibility. The comparison includes the signal's anti-alias filter and the selected Hann FFT window, without fitting its amplitude. Narrow theoretical plasma resonances are integrated before applying the window. The finite PIC box, computational particles and temperature controls can cause differences from this reference. See Rosenbluth and Rostoker (1962) for unequal-temperature theory. Linear spectrum shows power density on a linear axis, not voltage amplitude.

Plasma parameters

  • \(T_e = 2{,}000\,\mathrm{K}\) — electron temperature: describes the electrons’ random thermal motion, measured relative to their mean flow.
  • \(T_i = 1{,}000\,\mathrm{K}\) — ion temperature: describes the O⁺ ions’ random thermal motion. Their much larger mass makes their typical thermal speeds lower than those of the electrons.
  • ne = 10¹⁰ m⁻³ — electron number density: the number of physical electrons per cubic metre. The mean ion density is equal to it for this singly ionized, charge-neutral plasma.

These are the initial simulation parameters, not adjustable display controls or temperature measurements of the current frame. The particles and fields evolve during the recorded burn-in and the recording. Temperature measures random kinetic energy; the number of dots shown is only a display sample and does not set \(n_e\).

Numerical method

  1. Deposit the particle charges on the 128×128 mesh with quadratic triangular-shaped-cloud (TSC) weights.
  2. Fourier-transform the charge density. For every nonzero wave vector, solve \(\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf k) = \widehat{\rho}(\mathbf k)/(\varepsilon_0 |\mathbf k|^2)\). Set the spatially constant potential to zero.
  3. Obtain the electric field spectrally, gather it to the particles using TSC, then advance their velocities and positions with the explicit leapfrog scheme.
  4. Wrap positions at the boundaries of the full periodic square, and repeat.

The real-grid Nyquist derivative is set to zero. This recording uses the large explicit GPU solver, not the separate experimental implicit solver. Explicit PIC has numerical energy drift; the measured drift for this recording is being loaded. Energy conservation alone would not prove the spectrum is converged.

Recording and playback

This is a recorded GPU simulation after a recorded burn-in, not a new browser simulation. Initially, positions were uniform and velocities Maxwellian, with their species means removed and variances set to the target temperatures. After initialization the particles interact through the PIC field; their trajectories are not ballistic Maxwellian animations. The burn-in duration is stated, not claimed to prove equilibration.

For display, a fixed pool of recorded particle IDs per species was saved from the much larger population. The web export retains the available visible tracks from that pool, up to 500 per species at each frame. The selected run’s full and saved populations are listed above. True next-frame endpoints are retained, so interpolation never joins different particles. The slider only reduces this display sample; it does not change the field or signal. The recording cadence is being loaded; positions use shortest-periodic-path interpolation and potential uses linear time interpolation. The potential retains native grid points around the camera view, with lossy scalar JPEG compression for display. Short field and particle chunks are fetched on demand and only three decoded chunks are cached. Colours and contrast remain adjustable; the original scientific data are unchanged. None of these interpolated frames are additional PIC steps.

At 1× playback, one second on screen represents being loaded. Electron motion can become visually aliased at higher playback speeds; slow down or scrub to inspect it. Ions and electrons are never given separate clocks. At the end, playback stops rather than inventing a continuous loop.

Scattering calculation

In the single-scattering, plane-wave approximation, each electron contributes a phasor whose phase depends on its position along the Bragg wave vector. The displayed waveform is a normalized complex baseband envelope, not the 440.2 MHz carrier or a calibrated voltage in volts. The GPU evaluates the electron-density Fourier mode on the mesh, correcting for the TSC assignment window at that mode. This approximates the direct particle sum above. All 131,072 computational electrons contribute, including electrons outside the camera view. Ions influence the signal by changing the self-consistent electron dynamics; their dots are not separately added as electron scatterers.

Spectrum calculation

Rust compiled to WebAssembly calculates the FFTs from the same run's full recorded complex voltage record. The browser progressively averages Hann-windowed, 50%-overlapping periodograms. It shows a two-sided power spectral density in Hz⁻¹, with no per-window mean subtraction. Recalculation reprocesses this finite record; it does not create new independent samples. Overlapping windows are not independent realizations.

Before downsampling, the signal is low-pass filtered with a Blackman-windowed sinc FIR; edge transients are discarded. The waveform marker uses this filter's actual time offset. The spectrum uses the longer voltage record, while the dense particle/potential movie covers only its initial part. The ion-band and wide-band views show the same spectrum with different frequency axes. This is one short, finite-macroparticle realization—not an ensemble-converged ISR measurement, and there is no receiver-noise or instrument model.

The plasma-line plot shows the full spectrum on a MHz axis, including the ion line at zero frequency and the electron-frequency response on either side. The low-density run has broad resonances; the higher-density run can be inspected separately. Line widths and powers in these finite-PIC recordings are not claimed to be converged. No frequencies are removed from this plot.
