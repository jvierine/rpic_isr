The scattered-signal plot overlays a slowly varying, low-pass-filtered complex envelope (thick lines) on the full signal (thin lines). Blue is real and orange is imaginary. A symmetric Gaussian FIR has a 30 kHz −3 dB power cutoff and no phase delay. Rust/Wasm applies linear FFT convolution to the recorded signal; it does not change the particles, fields, or spectrum calculation. The unsupported filter edges are omitted rather than padded or wrapped. This separates the slow ion-band component from fast electron-frequency oscillations without claiming a perfect physical decomposition.

Plasma parameters

  • \(T_e = 2{,}000\,\mathrm{K}\) — electron temperature: describes the electrons’ random thermal motion, measured relative to their mean flow.
  • \(T_i = 1{,}000\,\mathrm{K}\) — ion temperature: describes the O⁺ ions’ random thermal motion. Their much larger mass makes their typical thermal speeds lower than those of the electrons.
  • ne = 10¹⁰ m⁻³ — electron number density: the number of physical electrons per cubic metre. The mean ion density is equal to it for this singly ionized, charge-neutral plasma.

These are the initial simulation parameters, not adjustable display controls or temperature measurements of the current frame. The particles and fields evolve during the 10 ms burn-in and the recording. Temperature measures random kinetic energy; the number of dots shown is only a display sample and does not set \(n_e\).

1. Two species, one self-consistent electric field

The full simulation has 131,072 computational electrons and 131,072 computational O⁺ ions. Each represents many physical particles. \(T_e = 2{,}000\,\mathrm{K}\), \(T_i = 1{,}000\,\mathrm{K}\) and ne = 10¹⁰ m⁻³. Both position components and both velocity components evolve. There is no magnetic field, no imposed collision operator and no externally driven field.

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm d\mathbf{x}_s}{\mathrm dt} &= \mathbf{v}_s, & \frac{\mathrm d\mathbf{v}_s}{\mathrm dt} &= \frac{q_s}{m_s}\,\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}_s,t),\\[0.6em] \mathbf{E} &= -\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi, & \nabla^2\phi &= -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0},\\[0.6em] \rho &= e(n_i-n_e). \end{aligned}\]

Here \(\mathbf x\) and \(\mathbf v\) are two-component vectors, \(q_e = -e\) and \(q_i = +e\). \(\phi\) is electric potential [V], \(\mathbf E\) is electric field [V/m], and \(\rho\) is charge density [C/m³]. The physical mass ratio is used for singly charged oxygen ions.

2. What happens at each PIC step?

  1. Deposit the particle charges on the 128×128 mesh with quadratic triangular-shaped-cloud (TSC) weights.
  2. Fourier-transform the charge density. For every nonzero wave vector, solve \(\widehat{\phi}(\mathbf k) = \widehat{\rho}(\mathbf k)/(\varepsilon_0 |\mathbf k|^2)\). Set the spatially constant potential to zero.
  3. Obtain the electric field spectrally, gather it to the particles using TSC, then advance their velocities and positions with the explicit leapfrog scheme.
  4. Wrap positions at the boundaries of the full periodic square, and repeat.

The real-grid Nyquist derivative is set to zero. This recording uses the large explicit GPU solver, not the separate experimental implicit solver. Explicit PIC has numerical energy drift; the measured drift for this recording is being loaded. Energy conservation alone would not prove the spectrum is converged.

3. What is the Debye-length window?

\[\lambda_{\mathrm{De}} = \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_0 k_{\mathrm B}T_e}{n_e e^2}}\]

For the selected run, the Debye length is being loaded cm. The view is 10\(\lambda_{\mathrm{De}}\) wide (being loaded cm), centred inside the full being loaded\(\lambda_{\mathrm{De}}\) periodic box. Its edges are not reflecting or periodic walls: particles simply cross the edge of the camera view. The physical size of a 10-Debye-length view changes with density.

4. Real motions, slowed down

This is a recorded GPU simulation after a 10 ms burn-in, not a new browser simulation. Initially, positions were uniform and velocities Maxwellian, with their species means removed and variances set to the target temperatures. After initialization the particles interact through the PIC field; their trajectories are not ballistic Maxwellian animations. The burn-in duration is stated, not claimed to prove equilibration.

For display, a fixed pool of 4,096 particle IDs per species was recorded from the much larger population. The slider chooses up to 1,000 visible particles from that pool, favouring the same IDs as they cross the view. It does not change the field or signal. The recording cadence is being loaded; positions use shortest-periodic-path interpolation and potential uses linear time interpolation. The potential retains native grid points around the camera view; no grid points are skipped. Interpolation between nodes is only for display. None of these interpolated frames are additional PIC steps.

At 1× playback, one second on screen represents being loaded. Electron motion can become visually aliased at higher playback speeds; slow down or scrub to inspect it. Ions and electrons are never given separate clocks. At the end, playback stops rather than inventing a continuous loop.

5. Why does the scattered signal fluctuate?

In the single-scattering, plane-wave approximation, each electron contributes a phasor whose phase depends on its position along the Bragg wave vector. At a monostatic radar frequency \(f_0 = 440.2\,\mathrm{MHz}\), \(k_{\mathrm B} = 4\pi f_0/c = 18.45\,\mathrm{rad/m}\). These phasors add coherently:

\[a(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_p}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_p}\exp\!\left[-\mathrm{i}\,k_{\mathrm B}x_j(t)\right]\]

The displayed waveform is a normalized complex baseband envelope, not the 440.2 MHz carrier or a calibrated voltage in volts. The GPU evaluates the electron-density Fourier mode on the mesh, correcting for the TSC assignment window at that mode. This approximates the direct particle sum above. All 131,072 computational electrons contribute, including electrons outside the camera view. Ions influence the signal by changing the self-consistent electron dynamics; their dots are not separately added as electron scatterers.

6. A spectrum calculated in your browser

Rust compiled to WebAssembly calculates the FFTs from the same run's full recorded complex voltage record. The browser progressively averages Hann-windowed, 50%-overlapping periodograms. It shows a two-sided power spectral density in Hz⁻¹, with no per-window mean subtraction. Recalculation reprocesses this finite record; it does not create new independent samples. Overlapping windows are not independent realizations.

Before downsampling, the signal is low-pass filtered with a Blackman-windowed sinc FIR; edge transients are discarded. The waveform marker uses this filter's actual time offset. The spectrum uses the longer voltage record, while the dense particle/potential movie covers only its initial part. The ion-band and wide-band views show the same spectrum with different frequency axes. This is one short, finite-macroparticle realization—not an ensemble-converged ISR measurement, and there is no receiver-noise or instrument model.

The separate plasma-line plot shows the electron-frequency part of the same spectrum on a MHz axis. It omits the central ±0.2 MHz so the ion line does not obscure the electron-frequency response. The low-density run has broad resonances; the higher-density run can be inspected separately. Line widths and powers in these finite-PIC recordings are not claimed to be converged. This gap is a display mask only: the signal and FFT are unchanged.

Controls

Pause / Restart / time slider: inspect the recorded interval. Visible particles: change only the number of dots. Species: isolate electrons or ions. Colour contrast: change only the symmetric potential display range. Negative potential is blue, zero is light yellow and positive potential is red. Values beyond the displayed limits saturate in colour, not in the simulation. FFT window: trade frequency resolution against the number of available averages.
